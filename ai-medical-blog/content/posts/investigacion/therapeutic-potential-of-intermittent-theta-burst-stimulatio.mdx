## Resumen

Intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS), a patterned repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation technique, modulates cortical excitability and induces neuroplasticity rapidly. Recently, iTBS has garnered considerable attention for its application in multiple central nervous system disorders. Its effects extend beyond local neuronal changes, propagating across synaptic plasticity, glial responses, neurovascular-immune microenvironment, subcellular homeostasis, and large-scale neural networks, though existing mechanistic evidence remains fragmented. Heterogeneous study designs, varying disease models, stimulation targets, parameters, and observation points, contribute to inconsistent findings. Most research only establishes correlations between iTBS and molecular, cellular, or network changes, failing to confirm causal sequences or cross-level relationships. This review synthesizes evidence from stroke, spinal cord injury, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and depression and anxiety-related disorders, analyzing shared and disease-specific mechanisms across biological levels. This review identifies limitations in causal evidence and dose-response characterization and proposes a multilevel framework for interpreting the reported biological effects of iTBS, with the aim of informing future clinical translation and the optimization of disease-specific stimulation strategies.
