## Resumen

High-throughput sequencing technologies have revolutionized virology in previously unimaginable ways, enabling rapid increases in the availability of viral genomic sequences by moving from culture-dependent methods to sequence-based pathogen detection and characterization methods. This revolution motivates the development of structured databases and new analytical frameworks to characterize the virosphere. Here, we focus on how virologists have integrated viral databases, bioinformatic and phylogenetic workflows, metadata standards, and the role of artificial intelligence (AI) in advancing knowledge of the evolutionary ecology of viruses and in using viruses as biocontrol strategies. Historically, viral databases have had an anthropocentric focus on human and clinical pathogens, creating inconsistencies in metadata for non-human viruses and at the expense of agricultural and environmental "dark matter" virosphere research. In response to the need to characterize the non-human virosphere, virologists are achieving major advances through the development and implementation of reproducible, scalable automated bioinformatic tools and phylogenetic approaches that depend on curated datasets, standardized pipelines, and transparent provenance. Characterizing the diverse non-human virosphere has opened new avenues for applications such as biocontrol strategies against fungal pathogens using mycoviruses. However, incomplete host-range data, the need for a holistic biological system, and the complexity of viral delivery systems often bottleneck these applications. Currently, virologists are adopting online platforms and AI approaches to enhance virus discovery, classification, genomic surveillance, and structural prediction, supporting virology and control applications. We highlight the importance of cooperation and research integration, describing the advances and challenges of integrating databases, workflows, metadata, phylogenetics, and artificial intelligence into unified platforms that support scalable, reproducible, and application-oriented virology.
