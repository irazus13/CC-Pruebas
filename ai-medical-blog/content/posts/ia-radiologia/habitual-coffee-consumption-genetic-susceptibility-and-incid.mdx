## Resumen

**BACKGROUND:** The association between habitual coffee consumption and incident stroke remains uncertain. **METHODS:** We analyzed 490&#x2009;889 UK Biobank participants without prevalent stroke. Average self-reported coffee intake across available assessments was categorized as &#x2264;0.5, 0.6 to 1.67, 1.68 to 3.0, and &gt;3.0 cups/d. Incident stroke was ascertained through linkage to hospital and death registries and self-reported physician diagnosis. Multivariable Cox models and restricted cubic splines characterized associations. Analyses assessed effect modification by CYP1A2 genotype and stroke polygenic risk score and used multiple-imputation and competing-risk sensitivity analyses. **RESULTS:** During follow-up, 22&#x2009;579 incident strokes occurred. Compared with &#x2264;0.5 cups/d, adjusted hazard ratios were 0.86 (95% CI, 0.83-0.89) for 0.6 to 1.67 cups/d, 0.92 (95% CI, 0.89-0.96) for 1.68 to 3.0 cups/d, and 0.98 (95% CI, 0.94-1.02) for &gt;3.0 cups/d. Splines showed a nonlinear J-shaped association, with the lowest estimated risk at &#x223c;2 to 3 cups/d. Associations were broadly consistent across CYP1A2 and polygenic-risk strata, with no significant interaction. Moderate intake was associated with lower risk among participants reporting sugar addition, whereas high decaffeinated-coffee intake was associated with higher risk. **CONCLUSIONS:** Moderate habitual coffee consumption was associated with lower incident stroke risk, with the lowest observed risk at &#x223c;2 to 3 cups/d. These observational findings should not be interpreted as evidence to prescribe coffee for stroke prevention.
