## Resumen

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a clinically important inherited myocardial disorder in sports cardiology because affected athletes may remain asymptomatic while being at risk of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac events. Cardiovascular screening, therefore, requires the identification of occult HCM while distinguishing pathological disease from physiological adaptations to training. This systematic review evaluated the diagnostic accuracy and external validity of artificial intelligence-enhanced electrocardiography (AI-ECG) for detecting HCM in athletes and sports-screening populations. PubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, Scopus, Web of Science Science Citation Index Expanded, Europe PMC, IEEE Xplore, ClinicalTrials.gov, and WHO ICTRP were searched from inception through June 2026, supplemented by reference lists and citation searching. The original June 2026 records formed the authoritative review corpus; no later search update was incorporated. Eligible studies evaluated AI or machine learning applied to ECG for the detection of current HCM in athletes against an imaging-supported reference standard. Risk of bias was assessed using QUADAS-2. The searches identified 1,024 database and registry records. After the removal of 321 duplicate occurrences and the addition of 18 records identified through supplementary methods, 721 records were screened, and 58 reports underwent full-text assessment. Fifty-six reports were excluded, one directly relevant study was ongoing without available results, and one published report was included in the qualitative synthesis. The included study evaluated 845 collegiate or professional athletes, of whom 99 underwent echocardiography, and one was identified as having HCM. AI-ECG-derived HCM probability was not significantly associated with echocardiographically verified HCM status (r = 0.173, p = 0.086). No prespecified HCM positivity threshold, HCM-specific sensitivity, specificity, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, or participant-level 2 &#xd7; 2 diagnostic data were reported, and no study met the prespecified criteria for quantitative synthesis. Athlete-specific evidence, therefore, remains insufficient to establish the diagnostic accuracy of AI-ECG for HCM or to support its use as a stand-alone rule-in or rule-out screening test. Prospective, participant-independent, imaging-verified external validation in representative athlete cohorts, particularly studies designed to distinguish pathological HCM from physiological athlete's heart, is required before AI-ECG can be considered a validated component of pre-participation cardiovascular screening.
