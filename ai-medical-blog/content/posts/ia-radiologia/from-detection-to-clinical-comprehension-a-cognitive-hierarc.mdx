## Resumen

Artificial intelligence (AI) detects findings in medical images at a level that, for specific tasks, matches or exceeds that of an expert radiologist. Detecting a finding, however, is not the same as understanding a disease. AI radiology literature rarely distinguishes between these two processes. This article proposes a four-level cognitive hierarchy, inspired by Ackoff's [1] model of data, information, knowledge, and wisdom, to analyze at which level current AI operates and its epistemic and ethical consequences. The four levels, detection, classification, diagnosis, and clinical comprehension, form a gradient of increasing cognitive demand. The last of these corresponds to the tacit knowledge described by Polanyi [2] and is consistent with classic studies of radiological perception. Recent literature on epistemic authority in medicine suggests that the gap between this level and AI reflects, at least in part, a difference in the type of justification underpinning clinical judgment. AI reliably automates the first two levels, makes partial progress on the third when structured clinical data are available, and currently lacks an operational counterpart for the fourth. This distinction has implications for the training of specialists and for clinical responsibility, which remains with the radiologist in AI-assisted diagnosis.
