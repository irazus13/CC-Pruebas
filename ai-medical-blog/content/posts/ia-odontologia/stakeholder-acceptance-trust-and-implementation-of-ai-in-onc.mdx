## Resumen

**BACKGROUND:** AI is entering oncology workflows faster than evidence on stakeholder acceptance, trust, and sustained use. Existing syntheses rarely distinguish anticipated attitudes from actual use across oncology stakeholders. **OBJECTIVE:** The aim of the study is to synthesize primary empirical evidence on stakeholder acceptance, trust, and implementation of AI in oncology and examine variation by stakeholder group, clinical task, AI exposure, and implementation maturity. **METHODS:** We conducted a Joanna Briggs Institute-guided mixed methods systematic review with convergent integrated synthesis; the protocol was registered before formal eligibility screening. Eight databases were searched without date or language limits (August 6-9, 2026), yielding 46,206 records and 31,802 unique records. Title and abstract screening used 2 balanced primary allocations, joint bidirectional readjudication, and blinded verification of a stratified 10% (n=3182) sample. Full-text assessment, extraction, and critical appraisal were dual independent. Acceptance/Trust modes and F1-F8 coverage were independently coded across 309 studies and reconciled. Eight integrated findings were anchored to the nonadoption, abandonment, scale-up, spread, and sustainability framework; an emergent economic finding was reported separately. Adjusted stage models controlled for study design, publication format, and publication period; robustness analyses examined screening provenance and appraisal sensitivity. **RESULTS:** We included 309 primary empirical studies; 187 (60.5%) were published in 2025 or 2026. Exposure ranged from hypothetical opinion to real-world implementation (n=82 studies), but only 6 described routine use. Acceptance was empirically assessed in 267 studies and Trust in 127. Relative to anticipatory stage 1, Acceptance assessment was less frequent in evaluated-interaction stage 2 (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 0.07, 95% CI 0.02-0.25; P&lt;.001) and stages 3+4 (aOR 0.12, 95% CI 0.03-0.45; P=.001). Transparency/explainability coverage (F3) was lower in stages 3+4 (aOR 0.20, 95% CI 0.08-0.47; q&lt;0.001). Workflow/interoperability coverage (F5) was higher and governance coverage (F7) lower in both later contrasts after false discovery rate correction. Trust declined descriptively across stages, but adjusted contrasts were nonsignificant. Eight integrated findings spanned clinical value, performance, transparency, oversight, workflow, readiness, governance, and implementation, plus an emergent stage 1 economic finding. The principal Acceptance, F3, F5, and F7 findings persisted in the 229-study original screening subset. **CONCLUSIONS:** In oncology, the AI evidence profile appears to differ from anticipated use to implementation. Acceptance was empirically assessed most often before deployment; later-stage studies more often examined workflow and less often examined transparency and governance. The challenge is whether favorable attitudes and early deployment translate into calibrated, governed, and sustained routine use. An interpretive stage-specific model identifies assessment priorities across exposure and implementation contexts, but is not a validated causal or predictive model. Later-stage evaluations should retain measurement of Acceptance, trust calibration, governance, durability, and workflow outcomes. **TRIAL REGISTRATION:** OSF Registries osf.io/nmep8; https://osf.io/nmep8.
