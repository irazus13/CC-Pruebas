## Resumen

**OBJECTIVES:** To distinguish what dental artificial intelligence (AI) models demonstrate from what their findings are claimed to mean, and to offer a framework for matching each claim to the evidence required to support it. **OVERVIEW:** AI studies in dentistry report sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve: measures of agreement between model outputs and a reference standard. Yet verbs such as predict, detect, identify, and explain are used interchangeably, and outputs are often read as identifying modifiable causes or as evidence of clinical benefit. Using the causal hierarchy of association, intervention, and counterfactual reasoning, this review separates description, prediction, causal effects of interventions, and individual counterfactuals. Directed acyclic graphs illustrate how confounding by indication can make an effective treatment appear harmful in a predictive model. The review summarizes the identification conditions that causal claims require (exchangeability, positivity, consistency) and where AI can legitimately contribute: measurement, nuisance-function estimation, heterogeneous treatment effects, and hypothesis-generating causal discovery. Model-explanation methods describe how a fitted model behaves, not how patients would respond to intervention. A published caries-detection model illustrates the evidence needed to move from diagnostic accuracy to claims about clinician decisions and patient outcomes. **CONCLUSIONS:** Most AI models developed for prediction operate at the association level. Model complexity and dataset size cannot substitute for a defined intervention, an appropriate design, and defensible identification assumptions. **CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE:** Dental AI accuracy studies show agreement with a reference standard, not that using the tool improves decisions or patient outcomes. Such claims require comparative evaluation of AI-assisted care.
