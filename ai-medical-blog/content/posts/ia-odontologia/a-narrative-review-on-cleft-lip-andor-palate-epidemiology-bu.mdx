## Resumen

Globally, cleft lip and/or palate (CL/P) affects 4.6 million people. CL/P occurs in approximately 1.4 per 1000 live births, making it one of the most prevalent congenital craniofacial anomalies. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is no exception, with cleft accounting for 13.11 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) per 100,000 population. This rate is 13 times that of North America. The effects of CL/P include feeding difficulties and problems with dentition, speech and facial aesthetics. It further affects patients' psychosocial functioning throughout their lives. However, short-term surgical missions are the prevailing model of care. About 68% of the cleft-related burden in SSA and South Asia could be addressed by surgery, yet comprehensive care remains an afterthought. This review summarises the broader epidemiological and pathophysiological evidence on CL/P. It also synthesises the psychosocial evidence and examines the various quality-of-life (QoL) instruments and utility-based measurement frameworks used in CL/P assessment. Instruments developed in high-income countries lack the languages and sociocultural constructs specific to West African settings. Furthermore, there are few or no preference-based utility values with that to conduct cost-effectiveness analyses for evidence-based decision-making. We argue that existing instruments are structurally unable to meet this need. Cleft-specific measures, such as CLEFT-Q and VELO, have no preference weights in any population, whereas generic preference-based measures omit the appearance, speech and stigma dimensions that dominate the burden. We set out the clinical, psychosocial and socioeconomic dimensions that a Ghana-sensitive index should value, how it should accommodate heterogeneity in cleft phenotype and functional impairment, and the role that artificial intelligence can legitimately play in its development. We conclude that a Ghana-sensitive CL/P utility index is a methodological and equity imperative, and that artificial intelligence can accelerate its development but cannot substitute for primary preference elicitation in Ghanaian populations.
