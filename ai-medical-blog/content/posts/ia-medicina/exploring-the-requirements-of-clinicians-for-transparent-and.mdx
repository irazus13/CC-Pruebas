## Resumen

**BACKGROUND:** Understanding how digital systems can support clinical decision-making is crucial, especially with the growing deployment of increasingly complex AI models. This complexity raises concerns about trustworthiness, affecting the safe and effective adoption of such technologies. In intensive care units (ICUs), where clinicians make high-stakes, time-sensitive decisions, decision support tools must be designed to align with clinical needs and cognitive workflows. A better understanding of decision-making processes and requirements for decision support tools is vital for providing effective solutions. **OBJECTIVE:** This study investigates ICU clinicians' decision-making processes, their use of decision support systems (DSSs), and the design requirements for DSSs to ensure transparent and trustworthy recommendations. **METHODS:** We conducted 2 virtual focus groups with 7 ICU clinicians from 1 hospital in September 2024. Participants included nurses, charge nurses, and medical doctors, each with at least three years of professional experience. The focus groups explored participants' perspectives on DSSs in relation to our study objectives. Reflexive thematic analysis was applied to the focus group transcripts, and analytic decisions were made inductively using open coding. Both semantic and latent coding were used. Following independent coding, a combined codebook was developed collaboratively, from which key themes and subsequent design recommendations were identified. **RESULTS:** Three core themes emerged from the analysis: ICU Decision-Making Relies on a Wide Range of Factors (theme 1); Complexity of Patient State Is Challenging for Shared Decision-Making (theme 2); and Acceptability and Usability of DSSs (theme 3). Five design recommendations derived from clinical input provide insights to inform future DSSs for intensive care: provide variable levels of detail (recommendation 1); support integration into existing clinical systems (recommendation 2); present explanations with additional contextual information (recommendation 3); prompt independent thought (recommendation 4); and facilitate communication of patient state (recommendation 5). **CONCLUSIONS:** DSSs have the potential to enhance ICU decision-making, but their adoption depends on alignment with clinicians' needs and workflows. To improve trust and usability, future systems must provide transparent recommendations, adapt to varying levels of patient complexity, and facilitate rather than replace human expertise. Our findings inform the development of transparent and trustworthy DSSs, which may improve their acceptance in ICU settings.
