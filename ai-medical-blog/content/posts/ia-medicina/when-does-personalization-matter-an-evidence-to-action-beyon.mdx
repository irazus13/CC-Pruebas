## Resumen

**BACKGROUND:** Precision medicine (PM) seeks to improve prevention, diagnosis, and treatment by incorporating biologic, clinical, environmental, and behavioral variation rather than relying exclusively on population averages. Medical technology (MedTech) provides a practical interface through which this approach can be translated into continuous sensing, decision support, and individualized intervention. We use the term personalized/precision medical devices (PMDs) for a conceptual class of devices that combine person-specific inputs with analytics and device-level configuration or adaptation. PMDs overlap with smart devices, connected health, and digital therapeutics, but are distinguished by their explicit coupling of individualized data to clinically meaningful device behavior. **METHODS:** This perspective uses a structured narrative literature approach. The literature survey was extended through targeted searches of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar, and major regulatory/standards sources for publications addressing precision or personalized medicine, biosensing, wearable and implantable devices, point-of-care diagnostics, automated drug delivery, digital therapeutics, artificial intelligence (AI), Internet of Medical Things (IoMT), interoperability, regulation, cybersecurity, and health equity [Oct. 2025-Oct. 2026]. Preference was given to peer-reviewed clinical studies, systematic reviews, authoritative regulatory guidance, and international standards. Evidence was synthesized narratively around a device lifecycle framework linking individual profile, sensing, data infrastructure, analytics, actuation, clinical workflow, evidence generation, and governance. **RESULTS:** Clinical evidence demonstrates that some components of the PMD concept are already established. In a randomized trial, automated insulin delivery increased time in the target glucose range by 14% points versus sensor-augmented pump therapy at 24&#x2009;weeks. Remote pulmonary-artery pressure monitoring reduced heart-failure hospitalization by 28% in a prespecified analysis of patients with reduced ejection fraction. In the Apple Heart Study, 419,297 participants were enrolled; 0.52% received an irregular-pulse notification, and atrial fibrillation was identified in 34% of participants who returned an analyzable electrocardiographic patch. Digital therapeutics and adaptive neurostimulation further illustrate how software and implantable systems can individualize treatment, although clinical generalizability and long-term evidence remain uneven. **CONCLUSIONS:** PMDs should be viewed as an engineering and clinical integration paradigm, not as a new regulatory category. Their value depends less on novelty of individual components than on reliable linkage among person-specific data, clinically validated algorithms, device actuation, and longitudinal feedback. Translation therefore requires evidence generation, interoperability, cybersecurity, lifecycle regulation, accountable AI, and equity-by-design. The next phase of precision MedTech will be defined by selective deployment of these capabilities where individualized sensing and adaptation demonstrably improve clinical outcomes or care efficiency.
