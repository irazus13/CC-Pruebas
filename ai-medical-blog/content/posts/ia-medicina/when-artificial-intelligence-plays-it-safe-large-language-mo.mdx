## Resumen

BackgroundIn acute abdominal pain, artificial intelligence may appear safe by preserving surgical sensitivity while increasing resource use through reduced specificity. We evaluated this safety-stewardship trade-off across frontier large language models (LLMs) to provide an exploratory diagnostic benchmark.MethodsWe performed a cross-sectional in-silico study using 50 standardized synthetic vignettes (25 urgent surgical, 25 non-urgent) internally developed by our investigative group. GPT-5.5, Claude 4.0 Opus, and Gemini 3.0 Ultra were evaluated using identical zero-shot deterministic prompting against an internally established, blinded dual-specialty expert consensus.ResultsExact primary-diagnosis accuracy was 92.0% (95% CI, 81.2-96.8%) for GPT-5.5, 88.0% (95% CI, 76.2-94.4%) for Claude 4.0 Opus, and 76.0% (95% CI, 62.6-85.7%) for Gemini 3.0 Ultra. Surgical sensitivity remained high across models (84.0% to 96.0%), whereas specificity was lower (64.0% to 80.0%) than specialist consensus. Models demonstrated a conservative escalation pattern with frequent over-triage decisions. Positive likelihood ratios ranged from 2.33 to 4.60. Over-investigation bias was the predominant error pattern.ConclusionIn this exploratory benchmark, frontier LLMs consistently prioritized surgical sensitivity over specificity in internally authored structured acute-abdomen vignettes. These findings reflect differences in performance on controlled synthetic scenarios rather than independently validated clinical diagnostic accuracy. Claims regarding clinical patient safety or readiness for implementation require future prospective validation with independent, real-world clinical cases and external multidisciplinary adjudication.
