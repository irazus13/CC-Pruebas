## Resumen

As a large-scale national utility provider, the organisation delivers digital services to a population of increasing diversity. Within complex, multi-brand environments, accessibility is frequently treated as a project-level requirement, applied after key design and engineering decisions have been made. This introduces inefficiencies, including duplicated effort, fragmented user experiences, and avoidable remediation. To address this, a centrally governed design system was developed and adopted as a shared platform across 80 products. The system encodes recognised standards, including WCAG 2.2 AA and EN 301 549, alongside internal organisational requirements, into governed and reusable assets. These include design tokens, patterns, and accessible components, ensuring that accessibility requirements are addressed systematically rather than through repeated project-level interpretation. The architecture was shaped and is maintained by a multidisciplinary team with diverse cognitive profiles, identities, lived experience, and levels of seniority. This diversity of perspective informed key design decisions, including the strategic positioning of Universal Design as infrastructure rather than retrospective adjustment. Outcomes were assessed using internal programme data, independent audit evidence, publicly available monitoring data, support-channel analysis, and a scenario-based environmental estimate. Delivery duration for comparable initiatives reduced from approximately 52 weeks to 10 weeks. Accessibility results improved within a range of 36% to 73%, based on sampled data. Support queries declined by approximately 30% following user-experience improvements, while a representative platform upgrade scenario produced an estimated carbon reduction of 75%. The findings indicate that governed Universal Design infrastructure can support more inclusive, efficient, and environmentally sustainable digital delivery, while continuing to require product-level accountability, testing, and governance.
