## Resumen

Advances in brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) and artificial intelligence enable the identification of neural signatures-patterns of brain activity linked to thoughts, emotions, and mental states. Next-generation BCIs, including adaptive deep brain stimulation, promise personalized treatment for movement and psychiatric conditions. However, their effectiveness depends on whether neural signatures accurately reflect individuals' experiences and clinical states. Because neural signatures are abstract, they require meaningful correlations with behavior, symptoms, and clinical outcomes. Ethical challenges arise when brain data, behavioral observations, and patient self-reports conflict, raising questions about whose knowledge matters and how to determine clinical "ground truth." We argue that neural signature research needs epistemic duty-the responsibility to make well-reasoned decisions using the best available evidence-and propose three strategies to address these challenges: contextual, dimensional approaches to integrate diverse data; involving patients in decisions about data use; and stress-testing neural models to assess their robustness under uncertainty and conflicting evidence.
