## Resumen

Clinician educators occupy a demanding role at the intersection of clinical practice, teaching, and scholarship. Given their teaching roles, they are expected to contribute in particular to health professions education scholarship, yet many have little protected time. AI tools have emerged as practical support for 2 of the time-intensive aspects of scholarly activities: conducting early-stage literature reviews and improving scholarly writing. The rapidly expanding AI landscape has made it increasingly difficult to distinguish purpose-built scholarly platforms from general-purpose AI assistants. As a result, many clinician educators remain uncertain about which tools are appropriate for academic work and how they can be used responsibly. Additionally, the barriers are often as much institutional as individual. Limited protected time, access to experienced mentors, methodological expertise, editorial support, and library services all shape the ability of clinician educators to engage in scholarship. Although AI tools may reduce some of the burdens associated with literature review and writing, they should not be viewed as substitutes for these institutional supports. This viewpoint is intended for clinician educators seeking practical guidance on integrating AI into health professions education scholarship. Our aim is not to provide a systematic review, an exhaustive list of platforms, or an endorsement or ranking of any platform, but rather to offer a practical overview of selected AI tools that we have found useful for health professions education research. These tools include Consensus, Elicit, Scite, Litmaps, ResearchRabbit, Paperpal, Jenni AI, Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, and Microsoft Copilot. We group these tools into 6 functional categories and discuss their strengths and limitations across different stages of the scholarly workflow, drawing on vendor documentation and our own experience rather than formal comparative testing, while emphasizing the importance of selecting tools that align with specific scholarly tasks. Since overreliance on AI risks diminishing creative scholarly competencies, we distinguish productive cognitive support, in which AI reduces routine workload, from epistemic off-loading, in which interpretation and judgment are delegated to AI, and argue for using AI as a scaffold that enhances, rather than replaces, intellectual engagement. We also address some ethical considerations, including hallucinated content, citation inaccuracies, transparency in disclosure, and subscription costs. Ultimately, responsible integration of AI into scholarship will require not only appropriate tool selection but also the continued cultivation of the critical thinking and scientific reasoning that define academic scholarship.
