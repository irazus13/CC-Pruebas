## Resumen

**BACKGROUND:** Generative AI is part of everyday study in the health professions. Safe use is thought to depend less on how much students use it than on how they handle its output: checking it, reasoning soundly when it is wrong, and keeping confidence aligned with accuracy. Which behaviors are associated with better performance is untested in physiotherapy education. **OBJECTIVE:** We tested (1) whether status in a structured "AI in Healthcare" elective (no course exposure, currently taking, or completed) was associated with performance on a clinical case examination (basic knowledge, case-based reasoning, and hallucination-trap items) and with metacognitive calibration, and (2) among students who used AI during the examination, whether self-reported cross-checking of AI output or the quantity of AI use was associated with performance. **METHODS:** In this cross-sectional study, 137 (71%) of the 193 invited physiotherapy students (no course exposure: n=67; currently taking: n=43; completed: n=27) completed a researcher-developed 30-item open-resource examination with item-level confidence ratings and an AI-use survey (cross-check frequency; number of AI-assisted questions). Prespecified primary outcomes were the hallucination-trap score, case-based reasoning, and the calibration gap. The per-participant Brier score was the confirmatory calibration test. Group contrasts used Kruskal-Wallis tests and heteroscedasticity-consistent regression adjusted for age, year, grade point average, and prior AI use. Among 108 AI users, cross-check frequency and AI-use quantity were modeled jointly, then adjusted for demographics, course group, and basic-knowledge score. **RESULTS:** Cross-check frequency was associated with total score (&#x3b2;=2.98, 95% CI 1.27-4.68; P&lt;.001), persisted after adjustment for demographics and course group (&#x3b2;=2.81, 95% CI 1.26-4.36; P&lt;.001) and after excluding fast completions, and attenuated once basic-knowledge score was added (fully adjusted &#x3b2;=1.08, 95% CI -0.09 to 2.24; P=.07). AI-use quantity was not positively associated with performance (&#x3b2;=-0.26, 95% CI -0.53 to 0.00; P=.05). Calibration differed by group on the per-participant Brier score (H2=10.23; P=.006) and expected calibration error (H2=12.31; P=.002), both worst in the completed group (calibration gap: &#x3b2;=0.078, 95% CI 0.023-0.132; P=.005; accuracy at maximum confidence: 70.5%, 95% CI 44.8%-93.2%; 61 responses from 14 participants). Currently taking students outscored no-course-exposure peers on case-based reasoning (&#x3b2;=1.01, 95% CI 0.21-1.81; P=.01) and hallucination-trap items (&#x3b2;=0.92, 95% CI 0.06-1.79; P=.04), but not after excluding 11 completions under 5 minutes; basic knowledge did not differ (P=.74). **CONCLUSIONS:** In an open-resource clinical examination, performance was associated with how students verified AI output, not how much they used it. The clearest deficit was metacognitive calibration rather than accuracy. These prespecified associations identify verification behavior and confidence calibration as measurable candidate targets for AI-literacy instruction. Because the design is cross-sectional and verification was self-reported, their instructional value requires testing in randomized or longitudinal designs.
